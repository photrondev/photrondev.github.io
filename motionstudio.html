4. Step 2, Points

Most applications name their points, and the pane asks for them in order: e.g. Gauge top and Gauge bottom for material testing, Object and Second point for a drop, Hip, Knee, Ankle, Toe, Trunk for biomechanics, Centre and Rim mark for rotating machinery, Projectile for ballistics. Click each on the frame, then drag to fine-tune with the loupe.

  • Auto-pick best features places the most trackable spots (strong corners, no repeating patterns, spread over the frame). How many points sets the count.
  • Auto points in box (): drag a rectangle on the frame; its most trackable features join the active body. Keep the box on the object only.
  • Add grid points (Material testing) keeps the gauge points and adds distinctive features in between: the grid behind the strain map.
  • Bodies (Crash): every point belongs to a body (Head, Chest, Pelvis, Sled reference or one you add with New body). A body holds any number of points; its position and rotation come from all of its sure points together. Click a body chip to make it active; select a point to rename it or move it to another body.
  • Tracking settings come from the application and can be changed: Method (Auto, the default, picks one at each Track; or template matching, optical flow, marker centroid and, when their packages are installed, the AI box trackers: see Trackers), Group model (none, auto, similarity, affine, perspective: points that move together help each other through blur and occlusion), and Template / search size in pixels.

Time & scale

The Time & scale card decides what the numbers mean. Both choices show as chips in the top bar and are saved with the studio.

  • Capture rate. A Photron file states it (From the Photron file). An MP4 only gives its playback rate, so enter the camera's capture rate; otherwise times, speeds and accelerations are in playback time and the results say so. Vibration, Rotating machinery, Ballistics and Biomechanics refuse to give results without it.
  • Scale:
    • The clip's calibration, made in Knowledge Studio > Describe > Calibrate (lens correction included);
    • Two points a known distance apart;
    • From gravity (Drop & impact): the object's free fall against 9.81 m/s², stated as an assumption;
    • From the projectile (Ballistics): its stated diameter, measured edge to edge on one frame with the ruler button and the loupe;
    • From a segment (Biomechanics): a stated segment length, e.g. the shank, over its median tracked length;
    • Pixels: no scale.
    The scale's own uncertainty is carried into every scaled value.
  • Time zero at frame: the frame where t = 0, e.g. the moment of impact.

The trackers: how they work, what they are good at

The Tracker card on the Track step picks the method. New sessions start on Auto (recommended): when you press Track, the studio takes a quick look (well under a second) at the placed points over the next 10 frames and picks the method, then says why in one line, e.g. Auto chose Template: textured targets, steady look. Printed dots on a plain background get Marker centroid; points whose look changes in a short template trial (rotation, blur, cover) get NanoTrack when its package is installed (up to 12 points; otherwise Template with a looser look-alike margin); large moves between frames, or many points on smooth texture, get Optical flow; anything else keeps the application's own method. The choice is made again at every Track. Choosing a method yourself in the list overrides Auto; sessions saved before Auto existed keep their method.

Each application's own method suits it; you rarely need to change it. The speed tag is measured end to end, decoding included (28 points on a 1280 × 674 clip): Fast ≥ 200 fps, Medium 60–200, Slow < 60.

Auto points in a box with Quadrant target or Circle target finds the marks themselves (not corners): every clearly symmetric mark in the box, best first; dots, thin lines and speckle are left out for quadrants, quadrants for circles. On the NHTSA sled clip it finds the eight marks on the dummy's head in one click.

Use an AI box method only when the target turns over, tumbles or changes its look so much that template and optical flow lose it; for measurement on a target that keeps its look, template and optical flow are over ten times more accurate (under 0.1 px on the synthetic tests). Both run on the GPU following the FP16 setting (without a GPU NanoTrack uses every CPU core, 29 fps with 28 points). Each appears in the Tracker card only when its optional feature package (NanoTrack, DaSiamRPN) is installed; a saved session that used a missing one opens with Template.

Group model: points of one body move together. Similarity (move, turn, scale), affine (plus shear) or perspective fit all the body's points each frame; a point that disagrees is rejected, a hidden point is placed by the others (shown as placed by the group), and the fit's rotation and scale turn each patch so matching keeps working. Each point alone uses no fit (independent points, e.g. droplets).

Advanced values (changes apply to the next track run; Defaults restores the application's values):

  • Template (px): patch size, odd. Bigger is steadier; smaller follows fine detail. For optical flow it is the flow window.
  • Search (px): area searched around the predicted position. Must cover the largest move between two frames; bigger is slower and finds more look-alikes.
  • Min confidence: below this match score (1 = identical patch) a point is uncertain and its frame is left out of the results.
  • Template update: 0 always matches the first patch (no drift). Above 0 the patch slowly adapts to a target whose look changes (a burning or deforming part), but long tracks can drift.
  • Predict motion: search where constant velocity predicts the point, not where it was. Keep it on for anything that moves fast.
  • Optical flow only: Pyramid levels (coarse-to-fine; each level doubles how far a point may move between frames, default 3), Back-check (px) (each point is tracked forward and back; one that does not return within this distance loses confidence, default 1 px), Iterations (refinement steps per level, default 30).
  • AI box only: Box (px) (the box each network follows, at least 16 px: cover the part of the object, a bigger box is steadier) and Min confidence (the network's score); search, template update, look-alike margin and predict do not apply.
  • Template only: Look-alike margin (a second match within this score of the best makes the point uncertain, default 0.2; 0 turns the check off).

7. Multi-camera (3D)

Two to eight synchronized cameras see the same points from different directions. Camera Studio calibrates them as a 3D rig (each camera's lens, position and time offset, from a wand; see Camera Studio's 3D rig mode) and saves a .rig file. Motion Studio opens that rig, shows its clips side by side on one timeline, and keeps every point paired across the views. Results come in 3D: x, y, z in mm with ± for every point seen by two or more cameras, or per view in image pixels.

Open a rig

  1. On the Source step pick Multi-camera (3D). The list shows the rigs saved in Camera Studio (${assets}/rigs, with each rig's wand error) and two rendered demos with an exact calibration: the Demo 3D rig (an LED flash, a wand and a thrown ball) and the Demo: drill drop test (3 cameras), a photoreal test bay at 1,000 fps where a cordless drill with four printed bullseye targets falls 1 m onto concrete, bounces and tumbles (impact at 4.43 m/s; camera A is level, so −y is up and a bounce reads straight off the y chart). Camera Studio's 3D rig mode offers the same two demos for the wand calibration. Open rig... opens a .rig from anywhere in Assets; Camera Studio takes you there to calibrate a new one.
  2. The views appear in a grid (the layout that shows them largest), each with its camera letter and its time offset to camera A. Scrubbing, the arrow keys and Play move every view together: camera B's frame is camera A's frame plus B's offset. The table lists each camera's clip, frames and offset; − / + nudge an offset by one frame if a view looks early or late (the tag turns amber, and its tooltip keeps the rig's value). Check the sync by eye on an event every camera sees (the Demo's LED flash lights all three views on the same frame).
  3. The card shows the capture rate (from the rig), the common frames every clip covers (the timeline's range), and the calibration.
States you may see: Clip not found on a view (the clip moved): Locate... picks it again, and the other views keep working (two cameras are enough to go on). A view with a different frame rate or a different size than the camera was calibrated at gets an amber frame and a warning. Clips that do not overlap in time leave no common frames, and Next stays off.

  1. Click the point in any view. It gets a name (the application's next point name, or Point 1, 2, ...; rename it in the pane). Its epipolar line appears in every other view as an amber dashed line labelled from A: the point must lie on that line there. The line comes from the rig's lens and pose, so with lens distortion it is a gentle curve, drawn exactly.
  2. Find match (on by default after the first click) searches along each line for the same patch (normalised cross-correlation on the frame, a narrow band around the line, the patch taken at the exact mark, the peak fitted to a few hundredths of a pixel; about 40 ms) and proposes the mark: a dashed amber ring. Click it to accept, or click where the point really is. A view where no confident match exists says so instead of guessing.
  3. A click within the snap band (6 px each side by default) lands on the line; with two marked views the lines cross at the one place the point can be, and a click near the crossing lands on it. Near 0 means the marks are one 3D point; a few pixels means one view has the wrong feature (the chip turns amber). A point marked in one camera only reads 1 view. Track

Track runs the same tracker in every view, with the same settings (Auto included: Auto picks a method per view from a quick look at each), one frame of every view in turn, so all views fill in live. Points stay paired by name: a row always belongs to the same 3D point in every view. The Track pane shows, per view, the frames tracked, the states on this frame and the method used; per point, its chips on this frame and the reprojection error now and over the tracked frames (median / worst). A worst value of several pixels points to a frame where one view jumped to another feature. The lanes show one band per point with a thin row per view. Speed is shown as world frames per second with every view tracked.

A run only rewrites the rows of the points it tracks: a point placed later (say a part that comes into view on frame 500) keeps its own track when you track the others again from an earlier frame, and a key set on an earlier frame leaves a point that did not exist yet alone.

When a point leaves a view. If the object turns away from one camera or leaves its picture, that camera's tracker may slide onto the background. With three or more cameras the rig notices: the other views still agree on one 3D point, and this view's mark is more than 5 px away from it. After 3 frames in a row the point is marked lost in that view from its first bad frame to the end of the run (red in the lanes), the 3D result uses the other views, and a message names the point, the view and the frame. While it is unclear which view is wrong (two views, or a mark off along an epipolar line) the disagreeing marks are only made uncertain on that frame.

Results in 3D

Results opens on 3D (rig): on every frame, each point seen surely by two or more cameras is triangulated (a linear start, then the position that best fits all its marks). The left shows the 3D path: every point's path in mm, coloured by speed (scale on the right), the cameras as frustums (or as markers on the edge when they are off screen), camera A's x, y, z axes and a floor grid. Drag to orbit, Shift+drag to pan, the wheel zooms, double-click (or ) resets; the marker on each path is the current frame and follows the timeline and Play. The readout at the bottom left gives every point's x, y and z (mm, camera A's frame) on the current frame: scrub to read a height or a position (with camera A level, as in the drill demo, −y is up, so a bounce is the change in y). Views switches the left back to the cameras. The right shows the usual cards and charts in mm: displacement, speed (m/s) and acceleration (g) in 3D, each with ±, and Distance 1-2 in 3D (on the Demo's wand it reads its 500 mm). The time and CFC settings work as with one camera. The chips A, B, C switch to one camera alone, in its image pixels.

Where the ± comes from (the info line and the 3D position ± card show it):

  • Tracking noise, measured from the data: the marks of a point never meet exactly, and the spread of those reprojection residuals over all frames gives the noise in pixels (measured; with too few frames it falls back to 0.3 px and says assumed).
  • Each camera's pose as the rig's calibration knows it (Camera Studio's report).
  • The rig's scale: the wand's length sets the scale for every frame at once, so its tolerance (0.05 %) is one common factor: about 1.5 mm at 3 m from camera A. It moves absolute positions, but hardly displacements or speeds.
  • Where each mark was put: a click or a drop is only as precise as your aim, about a third of a screen pixel, so its uncertainty in image pixels is that divided by the view's zoom (zoom in to place a mark and it shrinks); a Find match proposal is good to about 0.05 px. A mark the tracker never re-centres keeps that offset for the whole track, and an offset along the epipolar line does not even show in the reprojection error, so it moves the point in depth: this part is propagated into the ± (not into speeds). The marker-centroid tracker re-centres every frame, so it adds none.

Checked against the Demo 3D rig's truth: with the exact cameras, the tracked ball is within 0.12 mm RMS and 1σ covers 73 % of the frames (68 % is ideal). With a rig calibrated through Camera Studio's wand, the error is 0.45 mm RMS (a common scale of 0.015 %); the stated ± is cautious there, because it carries the wand's full stated tolerance. Placed by hand in the studio (zoomed in, clicks with a person's aim, Find match, snap), both wand ends come out at 0.6 mm RMS with 1σ covering 68 %. A point seen by only one camera has no 3D value: it is named in the info line, never guessed. Export CSV and Excel gain z and the ± per axis; the PDF report adds the 3D path view as shown.

The wand's markers: Camera Studio finds each wand marker's centre from the pixels above a floor at 10 % of the marker's contrast, so a rod touching the marker (a few grey levels brighter than the wall) no longer pulls the centre toward the other marker. On the Demo that pull read the wand 0.17 mm short and scaled the whole rig by 0.036 %; now the scale is within 0.015 %.
The session (rig, offsets you nudged, relinked clips, points with their marks per view, keys, snap settings) is saved with the workflow and comes back when the studio reopens; re-run Track to get the tracks again. Single-camera sessions are not affected.

Rigid body (6DoF)

When three or more points are seen in 3D and move as one rigid body (targets on the same part), Results 3D adds a Rigid body card: the body's rotation from the first frame (a total angle and yaw / pitch / roll), its centroid path and its angular speed, each with a ± from the points' own 3D uncertainty (Monte Carlo). The angular speed is taken over ±10 ms: frame to frame, a high-speed clip's rotation is smaller than the noise and would read too high. The card also shows how much the point-to-point distances change (rigidity); points that do not move together (a wand and a free ball) are refused with that reason, never fitted. On the drill drop demo with 0.5 mm of noise per axis, the peak spin reads 1,112 ± 31 deg/s against a true 1,107.

8. Flow & PIV (velocity fields)

Particle image velocimetry measures a whole flow field from frames of a seeded fluid (tracer particles in a laser sheet, smoke, fog). Pick Flow & PIV Studio on the start screen: instead of points, every frame pair gives a grid of vectors with an uncertainty each, and a run gives the mean and RMS fields with the uncertainty of the mean. The pixels run in the engine on the GPU (node cv.piv); Flow & PIV has its own six steps and no tracking transport. AI setup (top bar) proposes the settings: the engine probes a pair (particle size, seeding, largest shift, a static model), rules pick the window from the one-quarter rule and particles per window, the pair spacing for a slow flow, background subtraction and the auto mask; the AI reads only those numbers and your question (for example a stated reference length). The plan shows as changes with their reasons; nothing changes before Apply.

  1. Source. A clip from the workflow or Open... (MP4 or Photron MRAW). Pairs: Time-resolved (frame n with n+Δ, every frame; raise Δ for slow flows, aim for 5-10 px of shift) or Double-frame (A/B frames 0-1, 2-3, ...). Enter the capture rate (a Photron MRAW states it; the file's playback rate is not it) and the scale: the clip's calibration from Knowledge Studio fills it, or type a known mm/px. Vectors come in mm/s with both, mm/frame or px/s with one, px/frame with neither; the units show in the top bar. An optional reference length D (mm) and reference speed U∞ give profiles in x/D and u/U∞.
  2. Prepare. Pre-processing in the engine, in this order, each 0 = off: Background subtraction (the minimum of N frames spread over the clip: removes the sheet's glow, reflections and the model), Clip bright (caps particles brighter than k × the median particle peak; off by default, it showed no gain on synthetic tests), Intensity normalisation (local min-max over a radius: evens out an uneven sheet), High-pass (for smoke or fog without a background). Mask: Circle (click the centre, then the edge) and Auto mask (the model and walls stay bright in the minimum image while particles move); masked windows get no vector, never an interpolated one. Raw | processed split: click the frame to move the divider. Image quality (this pair, after pre-processing): particle image diameter (2-3 px is ideal; below 2 px the sub-pixel fit locks to whole pixels), seeding density, particles per final window (at least 6-10) and saturated pixels.
  3. Evaluate. A live preview of the pair under the playhead (about 20-40 ms on the GPU): Vectors, Speed, Vorticity or Uncertainty. Click a vector to see its u, v, σu, σv, correlation peak and status (measured, replaced by the median test, or no vector), and its correlation plane (first pass, before window deformation) with the peak ratio: primary over secondary peak, above about 1.5 the match is unambiguous. Presets with their measured bench accuracy: Fast (32 px, one pass), Standard (64 → 32 px, 3 deformation passes), Accurate (32 → 16 px), or Custom. Checks (in pixels per pair): the one-quarter rule (largest shift under a quarter of the first window), particles per window, the shift change across a window, and the smallest resolved structure (about two windows).
  4. Run. Set the frame range and Every (every k-th pair), then Run on the GPU; Cancel stops it. Click the frame to place a probe: its running mean with a ±2σ band shows convergence. The lane under the timeline colours each pair by its valid share (green ≥ 97 %, amber ≥ 90 %, red below). Speed is measured: pairs per second (decoding included) and the time per stage inside the node. Time-resolved runs with Δ = 1 decode each frame once (one reader; the node pairs the previous frame with the current one).
  5. Results. Mean (|mean| map with mean arrows) and RMS (√(u'² + v'²)). The card gives the field-average speed, the median RMS, the median uncertainty of the mean and the valid share; the probe's spectrum gives the dominant frequency (shedding, pulsation) with its resolution. Field movie: every pair of the run plays in the browser (no engine round trip), as a Vorticity or Speed map with Tracers carried by the measured field; click the flow to start a dye source (a streakline, like smoke in a wind tunnel), change the playback rate, zoom and pan while it plays. Line profile: click two points on the mean field for u, v and |V| along the line with the ±2σ band of the uncertainty of the mean (CSV); Streamlines of the mean field over the map.
  6. Export. The mean field, or every pair, as CSV, Tecplot (ASCII, ordered zone), VTK (ParaView, structured points with a velocity vector), MATLAB (.mat) or HDF5; every pair is one file per pair, or one stack in MATLAB / HDF5. Files go to a piv_export folder next to the clip. HDF5 needs the optional motion_studio package (Packages page); its button stays grey until then.

How good are the numbers

  • Per vector: correlation statistics (the spread of the correlation contributions around the peak) plus a term for unpaired particles (particles leaving the sheet): on synthetic pairs with 0-40 % particle loss, 1.2-3.5 px particles and 16 / 32 px windows the stated σ is 0.6-1.6 × the true error (correlation statistics alone read as little as 0.33 ×).
  • The mean: its uncertainty uses the effective number of samples: time-resolved pairs are correlated (lag-1 autocorrelation), so 400 pairs of a slowly changing flow count as far fewer. Run longer, or use Every, until the probe's band is narrow enough.
  • Spatial filtering: a window averages the flow inside it. Vortex cores and other structures smaller than about two windows are smoothed; the ± is the random error, not this bias. Use smaller windows (Accurate) where the gradients are strong and the seeding allows it.
  • Demo clip: demo_piv_cylinder_wake.mp4 (Assets > verticals) is a rendered cylinder wake (D = 10 mm, 9.4 m/s, 10,000 fps, St 0.21) with exact truth: a 60-pair mean matches the truth's mean over the same pairs to about 0.01 px per pair (median), and a 400-pair run finds the 197 Hz shedding at the probe within its 12.5 Hz resolution.

Stereo PIV: u, v and w from two cameras

One camera sees only the motion in the light sheet. Two cameras looking at the sheet from different angles also give w, the velocity through the sheet. On the Source step choose Stereo (2 cameras) and pick a rig calibrated in Camera Studio (3D rig), or the demo rig.

  1. The sheet plane. With no extra target, the plane goes through the point where the two optical axes come closest, square to their bisector. The pane shows the common area both cameras see, in mm, and the grid's mm/px; the capture rate comes from the rig.
  2. Self-calibrate (recommended). The two cameras' views are dewarped onto the plane and correlated against each other at the same instant. If the plane is not where the sheet really is, the views disagree. That disparity gives the sheet's offset and tilt, and the plane moves onto it (two passes). The report says how far the sheet was from the rig's plane.
  3. Prepare / Evaluate. The engine dewarps each camera onto the common grid (one cubic remap inside cv.piv), the pre-processing stages run on both, and PIV runs per camera. In every window the two in-plane displacements give four equations for three unknowns (u, v, w), solved by weighted least squares. The view shows u and v as arrows and w as colour (red away from the cameras, blue toward them). Masks (Circle, Auto mask) are drawn on this sheet view and apply to both cameras: the auto mask finds each camera's static bright regions and carries them onto the grid through its dewarp map; masks follow the grid when self-calibration moves the plane.
  4. Run / Results / Export. Every pair of both cameras into the mean u, v, w with the RMS and the uncertainty of the mean per component (effective samples, as above); the mean field exports as CSV, Tecplot, VTK or HDF5 (with the motion_studio package) with the w columns. Pair spacing on the Source step pairs each frame with the 2nd-4th next one: on slow flows w is much clearer, velocities stay in mm/s.
  • The ±: each camera's correlation σ goes through the reconstruction. The fourth equation also measures the real noise: when the residual says the two views disagree more than their own σ explains (dewarping, the plane, the sheet's thickness), every σ is scaled up by it. On the demo the stated σ covers 66-70 % of the true errors (68 % expected).
  • Demo rig: demo_stereo_piv (two cameras at ±30°, 5000 fps, a vortex in a 3 mm sheet with a known out-of-plane pattern). The sheet sits 0.6 mm in front of and 0.5° tilted from the rig's plane on purpose. Self-calibration finds 0.57 mm and 0.52°. The u, v and w errors are 0.0016, 0.0014 and 0.0031 mm per pair (RMS) on displacements up to 0.9 mm. Without self-calibration they are 0.0036, 0.0016 and 0.0046 mm.
  • Real footage: Real data: PIV Challenge vortex ring is case E of the 4th International PIV Challenge (2014, La Foy & Vlachos, Virginia Tech): a laminar vortex ring in water filmed by Photron FASTCAM APX-RS cameras at 1000 fps, one camera normal to the sheet and one about 25° off-axis. Both cameras are calibrated from the plate images (7 depths, two levels), and the sheet starts at the plate. Self-calibration moves it 0.7 mm. 99.9 % of windows are valid in both cameras, and the two views agree to 0.05 px. The two cores have equal and opposite circulation (within 7 %), and Γ/ν ≈ 2500 against the stated Re ≈ 2300. With one camera normal to the sheet and small shifts, w is noisy in a single pair at a pair spacing of 1; the stated σw covers that scatter. Pair spacing (Source) of 3 frames halves the w noise and makes w per pair a coherent field (consecutive pairs agree, r = 0.79); velocities stay in mm/s. The mean w is a reproducible structure (even vs odd pairs agree, r = 0.92). Import: test_suite/piv_bench/import_piv_challenge_e.py.

3D particle tracking (Shake-the-Box): tracks in a volume from four cameras

PIV gives a velocity field in a sheet; 3D particle tracking follows every seeded particle in a volume, seen by four cameras, and gives each one's position, velocity and acceleration over time, each with its ±. Open the 3D particle tracking application (Flow & PIV Studio). Its steps:

Watch the 3D particle tracking tutorial (3:33).

  1. Source. A synthetic 4-camera volume with exact truth (IW's own renderer: choose the flow, the recording, the seeding in particles per pixel, the number of snapshots and the image size). Recordings: Time-resolved (equal steps), Variable time step (random steps of 0.25, 0.5 and 1), Double frame (2 pulses) and Four pulses (independent pulse groups, pulses 0.25 apart). Flows include a high-dynamic-range jet and a 2D Taylor-Green vortex whose exact pressure is known. Every quality number in the studio is scored against that truth and labelled as such. Calibrations import from the 4th PIV Challenge (C_cal.csv), OpenPTV (.ori, .addpar, the glass and water of parameters_ptv.par) and Camera Studio 3D rigs.
  2. Source: Recorded footage. Real multi-camera clips with their calibration, no truth. OpenPTV cavity: a real water-filled lid-driven cavity seen by four cameras through glass (5 frames, 1280 × 1024; the OpenPTV .ori / .addpar with glass 1.46 and water 1.33; the static background is removed). 4th PIV Challenge case C: four cameras, double-frame pairs at 0.01, 0.02, ... ppp (choose how many; the organisers' raw 16-bit TIFFs when on disk, else the mp4 copies). Open clips + a calibration file...: one clip per camera and a PIV Challenge style .csv or an OpenPTV folder. Play frames steps through the four cameras. The steps then show what can be measured: the given calibration's reprojection (target dots or calibration points), particles found per camera, triangulated and reconstructed counts, tracks or pairs (case C: against the stated seeding), |v| and position σ; for the cavity, OpenPTV's own result on the same frames (particles, links, displacement and reprojection to the image particles, with the same calibration). The green marks in Camera images are the particles on tracks. Bin to grid and the pressure are off for the 5-frame cavity (they need a long recording); the pressure is off for case C (two pulses give velocity only).
  3. Calibrate & self-cal. The camera models are pinhole + lens (OpenCV), the Soloff polynomial (cubic in x and y, quadratic in z) and a flat glass wall into water (exact Snell ray tracing). Self-calibrate triangulates sparse particles with a wide allowed error, measures each camera's disparity in 5 × 5 × 3 sub-volumes and folds it into the camera's correction grid until it is under 0.05 px.
  4. OTF. The particle image of each camera and sub-volume as an elliptical Gaussian, fitted by the engine to isolated reconstructed particles. The shaking projects particles with it.
  5. Track. The engine finds sub-pixel peaks, triangulates them (every camera, then with one camera left out) and runs IPR: each particle is shaken in 3D to fit its projected OTF to the residual images, weak ones are removed, and new ones are found in what is left. Shake-the-Box then works snapshot by snapshot: every track is predicted from its last positions, shaken onto the new images, IPR finds new particles in the residual, and new tracks start from four snapshots. The pane shows the share of true particles tracked, the ghost fraction, the position error and σ, |v| and |a| with ±, the time per snapshot and the track length histogram. The view draws the tracks in 3D, coloured by |v| or |a|: drag to orbit, the wheel zooms; Camera images shows the four cameras.
    Variable time step: predictions and track starts are polynomials in the real snapshot times, and the first link's search radius scales with the step, so a short step starts fast tracks and a long step gives slow particles displacement. Double frame: each pulse pair is independent: IPR on each pulse, pairs matched with the neighbours' displacement, a normalised median test removes the inconsistent ones, then IPR runs again on the residual with the pairs kept; two pulses give velocity (its σ from the pair-to-neighbour scatter) but no acceleration. Four pulses: the same with four-pulse tracks, quadratic in time, so acceleration too.
  6. Bin to grid. Every track sample into bins (a linear fit per bin): mean velocity, Reynolds stresses and TKE, each with its σ from the effective N (distinct tracks in the bin: the samples of one track are not independent), and the divergence residual.
  7. Dense field & pressure. One snapshot's tracks onto a grid (1.5 mm by default): a quadratic moving least-squares fit per node of the fitted track velocity and Du/Dt, then the pressure from grad p = -ρ Du/Dt by least squares over every grid edge (Hermite edge integrals; the Poisson solution with Neumann walls; zero mean). The σ goes through the same chain by Monte Carlo from each sample's track-fit σ and the local fit scatter. The Pressure view shows the mid-depth plane. This is a simpler stand-in for VIC+ (no vorticity-transport constraint), so its resolution is the grid, not finer than the particle spacing. Two-pulse recordings have no Du/Dt and so no pressure.
  8. Export. Tracks as VTK polydata (ParaView), CSV and an HDF5 track store (position, velocity, acceleration and their σ per sample); the binned grid as VTK and Tecplot.
  • The ±: each track is fitted with a sliding 2nd-order polynomial (7 samples). The position σ is the track's own fit residual; the σ of velocity and acceleration come from the fit's covariance. On the synthetic volume the stated σ covers 74 % (position), 63 % (velocity) and 71 % (acceleration) of the true errors (68 % expected).
  • How good (own renderer, noise-free, 2.6 px particle images, 4 cameras, 512 × 512): at 0.05 ppp Shake-the-Box tracks 98.8 % of the particles with 0.02 % ghosts and 0.046 voxel RMS position error; IPR alone on one snapshot finds 99.7 % with 0.2 % ghosts. Independent 3D truth needs the LPT Challenge data, so these figures stay "own renderer" until that quality pass.
  • Double frame, four pulses, variable step (own renderer, 0.05 ppp): two-pulse 98.5 % of the particles with no ghosts, 0.045 voxel RMS; four-pulse 97.5 %, no ghosts. A jet with a 15 voxel per time-unit core sampled at random steps: the time-stamped tracking keeps 99.6 % (core 99.95 %), where equal-step tracking on the same images loses the core (2 %).
  • Pressure (own renderer, 0.05 ppp, 12 snapshots): against the exact pressure of a Lamb-Oseen vortex and a 2D Taylor-Green vortex the RMS error is 0.25 % and 0.34 % of the pressure range, and the stated σ covers the true error at 72 % and 77 % of the nodes (60-80 % is the honest target). The dense velocity's σ adds the two truncation biases (the cubic vs quadratic fit in space and in time) to the robust local scatter and covers 75-77 % (vortex) and 62-75 % (Taylor-Green) per component.
  • Real images: 4th PIV Challenge case C (four synthetic cameras, exact calibration, 9 seeding densities as a/b exposures). The pinhole fit reproduces the calibration points to 5×10-7 px (Soloff 0.004 px). IPR reconstructs about the stated number of particles up to 0.05 ppp, and 96-100 % of them are found again in the b exposure; above 0.05 ppp single-snapshot ghosts grow, which is where tracking over time is needed. Two-pulse pairing on the organisers' raw 16-bit a/b exposures (the unpaired particles are dropped each round, so ghosts do not pile up): up to 0.1 ppp the pairs are 76-83 % of the stated count and 92-93 % agree with their neighbours' displacement within 0.5 voxel; at 0.125 and 0.15 ppp 65 % and 47 % are paired and 83 % and 72 % agree (position noise grows with image overlap). OpenPTV test_cavity (a real tank behind glass): the imported orientation with the glass and water puts the calibration target on the detected dots within the dots' own detection noise.
  • Speed: about 160 ms per snapshot at 13 k particles (512 × 512 × 4) on a laptop GPU, IPR on one snapshot 250 ms; the CPU build gives the same answer (the shake is one shared routine, parity under 10-4 voxel).

9. Strain & DIC (strain fields)

Digital image correlation measures displacement and strain over a whole surface from a speckle pattern (sprayed paint, a printed pattern, or the material's own texture). Pick Strain & DIC Studio on the start screen: every frame is compared with the reference frame (the undeformed state), and each point of a grid gets u, v, the Green-Lagrange strains εxx, εyy, εxy and an uncertainty. The pixels run in the engine on the GPU (node cv.dic: subsets matched by IC-GN with an affine shape function, zero-normalised SSD and cubic B-spline sampling); Strain & DIC has its own steps.

  1. Source. A clip from the workflow or Open.... Choose the reference frame (strain is measured against it), the capture rate (needed for a load file in time) and the scale (from the clip's calibration, or type mm/px). Strain needs no scale; displacements are in mm once it is known.
  2. Speckle. The pattern on the reference frame is checked: speckle size (3-7 px is ideal), mean intensity gradient (above about 20 grey levels per px gives noise under 0.01 px), saturated pixels (under 1 %: saturated speckles lose their shape) and contrast. Draw region (two opposite corners) keeps the points on the specimen; points with no speckle get no value, never an invented one.
  3. Subsets. A live solve of the frame under the playhead against the reference. Presets Fast (41 px subsets, step 16), Standard (31 / 10) and Fine (21 / 6), or set the subset (3-5 speckles across it), step (a third to a half of the subset), strain window (points in the plane fit: bigger is smoother and less local), largest motion (the first frame's search range) and minimum ZNCC. Show εyy, εxx, εxy, ε1, von Mises, u, v, |d|, ZNCC or σ; click a point for its numbers.
  4. Correlate. Run on the GPU over a frame range (every k-th frame if wanted): frames are solved in order, each starting from the previous answer. Speed is measured (frames and points per second, solve time per frame); the lane colours each frame by its valid share.
  5. Strain. A virtual strain gauge (draw a rectangle; the default is every valid point whose strain window is complete: at the region's edge the fit is one-sided and biased) gives the mean εyy, εxx, εxy per frame with ±2σ, and a virtual extensometer (click its two ends; each end averages a small patch of points) gives the engineering strain between them. The chart shows both over the run. Moving the gauge or the extensometer re-measures the stored fields: no new correlation.
  6. Tensile. A load-cell file (CSV: time in s or frame, then force in N or kN) and the cross-section give the stress-strain curve and, in the style of ASTM E8 / ISO 6892-1: Young's modulus E (linear fit over the first loading between 10 and 40 % of the tensile strength, ± from the fit), Poisson's ratio (−εxx / εyy over the same range), Rp0.2 (the 0.2 % offset line) and the tensile strength; then, for sheet metal, the strain hardening exponent n (ASTM E646 / ISO 10275: Hollomon fit of true stress against true strain from 10 % strain, or half the uniform elongation when that is under 12 %, up to the uniform elongation) and the plastic strain ratio r (ISO 10113: width over thickness plastic strain, thickness from constant volume, from the slope over the same range), each with ±2σ. Strain comes from the extensometer when one is placed, else the gauge. Forming limit (ISO 12004-2): on a specimen taken to fracture, Find the limit strains uses the last frame before the crack (valid points still at least 90 % of the run's median) and both ISO methods: the position method (true major and minor strain on 3 or more sections across the crack, an inverse parabola fitted outside the neck with ISO's window W = 10 mm (1 + ε2/ε1), its peak is the limit strain, ± from the fit, the window placement and the section scatter) and the time method (the onset of thinning instability at the neck). Add to the FLC keeps each specimen (name it, e.g. by its width) in dic_export/flc.json; with 3 or more the FLC is fitted (FLC0 ±), and This frame on the FLD plots any frame's strains on the forming limit diagram as fail / marginal (within 10 %) / safe / compression. On the real X6Cr17 coupon below, the limit strain is ε1 0.33, ε2 −0.16 (uniaxial side), and the two methods agree within 15 %.
  7. Export. Every frame's field as CSV, VTK (ParaView), one MATLAB stack or a UFF universal file (.unv: the grid as nodes and quads, then a displacement and a strain tensor per frame, for FE and modal tools), plus the series (gauge and extensometer per frame) as CSV, in a dic_export folder next to the clip. Three more tools on this step:
    • Quality report measures this run's own noise floor and spatial resolution: the reference speckle is shifted by a known sub-pixel amount and warped by sine displacements of shrinking period (fresh noise at the image's own level added), each solved with the run's settings. It gives the displacement and strain noise, the MTF (gain of u and ε against the period), the shortest period followed at 90 % (and 50 %) and the virtual strain gauge length. Type frames before the load (for example 0-4) to add the measured noise floor of the real clip. The report is saved as <clip>_quality.json. The image noise is estimated from one image and fine speckle adds to it, so the synthetic floor errs high.
    • Acceptance report (structured after VDI/VDE 2626): type the frames with no load (static noise) and the frames after a known rigid translation of the specimen with its nominal move in mm; it reports the displacement and strain noise, the rigid-motion displacement error and the strain that must be zero, the spatial resolution (after a Quality report) and, for a stereo rig, the reprojection error, each against a limit, with an overall pass / fail, as <clip>_acceptance.json and .pdf. It records the tests and numbers; it is not a certification.
    • Compare with FE reads FE nodal results (CSV with x, y, u, v in mm and optionally the strains, or a UFF) and places them on the reference image (origin in px and angle). The FE displacement is averaged over the same subset and its strain taken from the same plane fit as the DIC (levelling), so the difference shows the model, not the DIC's smoothing. It gives the mean and RMS of DIC − FE for u, v and the strains on the current frame. From Python, dic_features.register_fe_hook(name, fn) plugs in your own FE source.
    • Live gauge in the workflow adds a Movie Reader, a cv.dic node with this gauge and extensometer, and a Metrics Logger to the workflow. cv.dic outputs the gauge εxx, εyy, εxy, its σ and the extensometer strain on every frame (inputs gauge region and extensometer), so the gauge runs at camera rate with no studio open. Replace the reader with a camera node to measure live.

How good are the numbers

  • Per point: σ from the solver's Hessian and the residual noise. The gauge and extensometer ± also include what the solver cannot see (sub-pixel interpolation bias that swings slowly with the motion): each series' scatter about a local fit over ±12 frames raises the stated σ where it is bigger. On the demo the 2σ band covers 91 % of the gauge errors and 91 % of the extensometer errors.
  • Spatial resolution: a subset and the strain window average the field inside them. On the 2D-DIC Challenge Sample 14 (real benchmark images, a sine of 0.1 px amplitude whose period shrinks along x) a 31 px subset follows the truth to the images' noise floor (about 0.01 px) where the period is longer than 4 subsets, and reads 0.88 of the amplitude at 3-4 subsets: use smaller subsets (Fine) where strain changes quickly.
  • Demo clips: demo_dic_coupon.mp4 (Assets > verticals) is a dog-bone coupon pulled to fracture at 2000 fps with exact truth: elastic, yield, 14 % uniform stretch, a neck that reaches 59 % local strain, and fracture. Every point is tracked up to fracture, the uniform strain is within 20 microstrain of the truth, the neck's peak is read about 9 % low (the strain window averages it: use Fine where strain localises), and the Tensile step gives E 69.0 GPa, Poisson 0.32 and Rp0.2 277 MPa (truth 69, 0.33, 276); the strain map plays with the video after a run. demo_dic_tensile.mp4 is a rendered aluminium tensile test with exact truth (3 % strain at 500 fps, Poisson 0.33, a load file from E 69 GPa, Rp0.2 276 MPa): the gauge εyy is within 43 microstrain of the truth on every frame (noise floor about 1 microstrain on the static frames), and the Tensile step gives E 69.1 GPa, ν 0.336, Rp0.2 276 MPa.

Stereo DIC: u, v, w and the surface strain from two cameras

One camera sees only the motion in its image plane: a specimen moving towards it looks bigger, which reads as strain that is not there (on the demo dome, 11,800 microstrain where the truth is 10,100). Two cameras looking at the specimen from different angles also give w, the motion out of the surface, and the true surface strain. On the Source step choose Stereo (2 cameras) and pick a rig calibrated in Camera Studio (3D rig), or the demo rig. Import a calibration reads another system's stereo calibration into a rig: Istra4D (.isprp), VIC-3D (the calibration block of a .z3d, .xml or .cal project), DICe (xml) or an OpenCV stereo yaml. Give the two clips, and the first row kept when the clips are a crop of the calibrated sensor. The rig is saved in Assets > rigs with camera 1 as its world; the import reports the baseline and the angle between the cameras as a sanity check. VIC-3D's radial distortion terms are read as the r², r⁴, r⁶ coefficients: check the reprojection on a calibration image when the lens is strongly distorted.

  • How it works: both cameras are resampled by the engine onto one grid on the specimen plane (the dewarp map input of cv.dic); each camera is correlated in time against its own reference, and camera B's frames against camera A's reference give the stereo disparity: the specimen's starting shape (height z0 above the plane) and which point of B's field is the same material point as A's. Every point's two in-plane displacements give u, v, w by weighted least squares, and the strain is the Green-Lagrange strain of the surface in its own tangent plane, from the full 3D deformation (the stretching that bending or bulging adds included).
  • Self-calibrate (Source or Speckle step) finds where the specimen really is from the disparity and moves the plane onto it: on the demo it finds the plate 0.400 mm and 0.30° off the rig's plane (truth 0.4 mm / 0.3°). Do it before measuring.
  • Subsets solves the frame under the playhead in 3D and draws a map of w, εyy, εxx, εxy, u or v; Correlate runs both cameras; Strain gives the gauge (every valid point) and a 3D extensometer (click its two ends on the map): the true 3D distance between the two points. Tensile works as in one-camera mode, and adds a Vibration shape card: give a frequency (with the capture rate set) and the run's 3D displacement is locked in at it per grid point, drawn as amplitude and phase maps of w (out of plane), u or v on the specimen plane: the operating shape of a plate or panel in 3D. On a synthetic plate mode it recovers the shape exactly (MAC 1.0). Export: every frame's x, y, z0, u, v, w, strains and σ as CSV, or VTK with the deformed surface.
  • How good: on the demo (a plate stretched 2 %, moved 0.4 mm and bulged 1.5 mm towards the cameras, 8.5 px/mm) u, v, w are within 0.0009 / 0.0007 / 0.002 mm RMS of the truth, the surface strain within 0.0001 RMS, the gauge within 11 microstrain and the 3D extensometer within 15 microstrain on every frame. Points are triangulated exactly (each camera's ray through where it sees the point), so a large bulge is not under-read: on a 5 mm dome w stays within 0.004 mm. The stated σ is honest: over the run 2σ of w covers 94 % of the real errors. It adds, in quadrature, the correlation σ, the two cameras' disagreement, a first-order subset's bias on a curved surface and, when the rig carries a wand check (Camera Studio), the calibration's scale uncertainty times the displacement.

10. Markerless biomechanics (3D keypoints)

Biomechanics Studio's Source step has a third source, Markerless 3D: synced clips on a calibrated rig, no markers on the athlete. A keypoint model finds 26 body points in every view (Halpe-26: the body plus heel, big toe and small toe on both feet), they are solved in 3D with a σ each, a scaled leg model gives the joint angles with ±, and foot contacts come with a ± in ms. The keypoints run in the engine on the GPU (node cv.body_keypoints); Markerless 3D has its own steps.

Watch the markerless biomechanics tutorial (3:14).

  1. Source: pick a take, a rig saved in Camera Studio with its clips, or the COMFI validation takes (4 synced webcams with marker truth and force plates). Software-synced cameras (each frame with its own timestamp) are resampled to camera A's capture times before 3D.
  2. Keypoints: Find keypoints runs RTMPose-m (Halpe-26, SimCC) on all views in one batch per frame. The crop follows the last frame's keypoints, and a lost view is searched again with the same model (no person detector). Circles show 2σ of each keypoint. The speed chip shows the node's own frames/s and its backend. TensorRT builds an optional engine for this many views per pass (about a minute, once).
  3. 3D & model: each view is weighted by its keypoint σ. The σ is calibrated from the views' agreement on the take itself, so no truth is used, and a view that disagrees with the others (a left / right swap, a hidden limb) is dropped. The model has a pelvis with 6 DoF, a trunk (lumbar, with a weak prior: keypoints cannot see the spine), and per side a hip with 3 DoF, a knee with 1 and an ankle with 2. Segments are scaled to the athlete. The table lists the views and the 3D σ per keypoint.
  4. Events: foot contact and foot off come from the heel and toe heights in 3D (with hysteresis), timed between frames by a line fit. The ± is the height noise over the foot's speed. Import CSV or C3D adds force plates (Fz over 20 N = contact; centre of pressure) or EMG (band-pass, rectified, RMS envelope, onset with ±). The plate events are compared with the video events.
  5. Results: pick an angle to see it over the take, with its ±2σ band and, on a validation take, the markers. Headline numbers (peak flexions, flight time, jump height) carry their ±. On a take with marker truth, the validation table lists RMSD, bias and how often the stated σ covers the error. The pelvis is shown weak when its RMSD is over 5°. It is never hidden.
  6. Export: C3D (the 3D keypoints in mm and the plate's Fz), OpenSim .trc (keypoints as markers) and .mot (joint angles in degrees), an angles CSV with every σ, and an overlay MP4 of one view with the skeleton, the 2σ circles and the events. The C3D, TRC and MOT writers are IW's own.

How good are the numbers

The numbers below are measured on COMFI participant 1012 (LAAS-CNRS, CC BY 4.0): 4 software-synced webcams at 1280×720 and 40 fps, Vicon-type markers and 3 force plates. The lifting take calibrates the video-to-mocap lag (58 ms) and the ± model term. The jumping take is held out.

  • 2D keypoints: the engine matches onnxruntime on 200 crops (FP32, max 0.000 px; TensorRT max 0.25 px). The median error against the projected marker joint centres is 10-15 px. That includes COMFI's own camera-to-marker calibration offset (the markers project about 15 px off in one view) and the difference between a keypoint and an anatomical joint centre.
  • Speed (RTX 3080 Ti laptop GPU, 4 views, tracking on): 182 four-view frames/s with TensorRT FP32 (729 crops/s), 98 with OpenCV DNN FP32, and batching the views is twice as fast as one pass per view. FP16 moves keypoints by up to 1.4 px against the CPU (over the 0.5 px parity limit), so the node checks the precision on its first crops and keeps FP32 when FP16 does not agree. The 500 frames/s target is not reached on this GPU.
  • 3D keypoints: on synthetic skeletons with known per-view noise, the stated covariance is exact (Mahalanobis rms 1.00, 95 % ellipsoid 0.95). On COMFI jumping, after the camera world is registered to the marker world (fitted on lifting: 4.6° and 5 cm), the knees are 26 mm RMSE from the marker joint centres, the ankles 45 mm and the hips 39-57 mm. The stated 1σ covers 69 % of the errors.
  • Joint angles (jumping, held out, against the marker angles): knee 8.7-14.8°, hip 12.6-17.8° and ankle 5.2-5.4° RMSD. On the slower lifting take the knee is 4.3-6.0° and the ankle 4.7-7.8°. That is above the 3° target at this frame rate: the 4 webcams face the athlete front and back, so the sagittal depth is weak, and frames are motion-blurred at 40 fps. The stated ± covers 74 % of the errors on average. The pelvis tilt (7.5° RMSD) is flagged as weak.
  • Events against the 1 kHz plates (jumping: 5 takeoffs, 5 landings, all found): the errors are -25 to +20 ms with a stated ± of 8-24 ms (it includes the software timestamps' T/√12). 8 of 10 fall inside 2σ. Landings come about 15 ms early, because the 25 mm height threshold fires before the plate is loaded. The method works at 40 fps, but the ≤ 2 ms gate needs a high-speed capture against a plate. On synthetic contacts at 1,000 fps the stated ± is 0.55 ms, and it covers 66 % (1σ) and 93 % (2σ) of the errors.
  • High-speed accuracy is pending: the fine-tune pipeline (synthetic motion blur along each limb, motion_studio/keypoint_finetune.py) is built, but the claim needs a lab capture with 4 high-speed cameras, markers and a 1 kHz plate.
  • Licence: the default weights are MMPose RTMPose (Apache-2.0 code) trained on data with research-only terms (MPII, Halpe/AlphaPose). They are for internal use and demos until a legal review.

Spray & Droplet

Tracked drops give droplet speeds and direction; the liquid's envelope gives the spray width, penetration and cone angle per frame.

Droplet sizing (Results, Size the drops) runs every frame of the analysis window through the C++ Particle Sizer node: each drop's sub-pixel diameter from its shadow's coverage (the half-depth edge of shadowgraphy; a drop's bright lensing spot is filled), ellipse, roundness and edge focus, touching drops split, drops cut by the frame left out. The studio then keeps only in-focus, round drops (Min focus, Min roundness; ligaments and merged pairs fail roundness, blobs above Max size are the liquid core), removes repeated frames (a slowed clip repeats frames: each drop counted once) and specks on the window (a dark dot that does not move between captures), and gives:

  • D10, D32 (Sauter), D43 and the volume quantiles Dv10 / Dv50 (VMD) / Dv90 and the span, each with a bootstrap ± over the drops;
  • the depth-of-field correction: a shadowgraph sees big drops over a deeper slab than small ones, so each drop counts with 1 / DoF(d) (DoF proportional to d unless calibrated from a reticle traverse); the raw D32 is shown beside the corrected one;
  • the number and volume distributions (log size classes, D32 marked), size-velocity (drops paired across consecutive captures by position and size; the mean speed per size class ± its standard error; needs the capture rate) and the drops per frame;
  • Shape: the median aspect ratio (major / minor) and the share of spherical drops; across the spray: a grid over the frame with each cell's D32, drop count or mean speed;
  • with the scale and a calibrated depth of field: number density (drops / cm³), the liquid volume fraction and the volume flux carried by the paired drops (cm³ / cm² / s), over the region or the frame;
  • Calibration: Check on this frame sizes a reticle's dots against their nominal diameters (error % ± per size, the worst); Calibrate the depth of field from this clip takes a reticle moved through focus over the analysis window (its depth at the first and the last frame) and fits DoF = a + b × d, which the correction and the number density then use;
  • Outlines in the viewer shows each sized drop outlined and coloured by its diameter, specks on the window crossed out and rejected blobs ringed grey; CSV writes every particle of every frame.

Sizes are in pixels until the scale is set on the Points step. Tested: discs of known size within 2 % (0.3 px below 15 px) in shadowgraph and bright field, an inkjet's drops repeat within 2.5 % over their flight, about 100 000 drops a second on a dense 1280 × 800 frame.

15. The Multi-Point Tracker node

The studio runs on the C++ node Multi-Point Tracker (cv.multi_point_tracker, Tracking category, CUDA and CPU builds), which you can also use in any live workflow:

  • Methods: template matching (NCC, sub-pixel peak), pyramidal optical flow with a forward-backward check, marker centroid, and the AI box trackers NanoTrack (all points in one batched network pass) and DaSiamRPN (up to 8 points), each from its optional feature package.
  • Group model: 2 points similarity, 3 affine, 4+ perspective; only sure points vote, and hidden points are placed by the group and re-acquired.
  • World units: optional lens (a Camera Studio .calib) and world_homography inputs.
  • Outputs: a preview (trails, state colours, motion vectors, group outline), the points matrix for the current frame, and history: every frame since the restart, so a slow reader (logger, Live Sheet, Python) never misses a frame. history_frames caps it (default 10,000, 0 = off).
  • Studio mode: the frame and keys inputs let it track in either direction and restart from hand-set keys; with frame = -1 it runs live.

The tracker's fixed cost is under 1 ms a frame on both builds; the studio shows its tracking speed (fps best) after each run.

For box tracking in other workflows, Multi Box Tracker (cv.multi_box_tracker, NanoTrack feature package) follows N boxes (x, y, w, h) at once and outputs the boxes, a confidence per box and a preview; Start or new boxes restart it. Demo: demos/Inference/Multi Box Tracker.json.

16. Good results and limits

  • Use the camera's real capture rate. Copies from the web are often slowed or have repeated frames; times are only as good as the frame rate.
  • Choose points with texture or a marker, on the object only. Avoid repeating patterns, reflections and edges that slide (a turning helix, a limb's outline).
  • Check the lanes: long uncertain or lost stretches mean the point needs a key, a bigger template, or a different method.
  • A clip calibration is only correct on its plane: points in front of or behind it read too long or short.
  • One camera measures in the image plane (use Multi-camera (3D) for depth). Out-of-plane motion shortens lengths and angles; Biomechanics puts this in the ±.
  • A camera mounted on the moving vehicle or rig shakes: add a reference body (4-6 points on rigid structure, both sides of the frame) so its motion is subtracted. On NHTSA 11500 this cut the head's difference to the accelerometer from 15 g to under 6 g RMS.
  • Acceleration needs a clear view: compare video with a sensor only while the point is visible (on 11500, before the airbag covers the head, about 45 ms).
  • AI box trackers keep the point through rotation and blur but are box-level (about 2-3 px), not sub-pixel: prefer Template or markers for precise measurements.
Results are measurements, not certifications. Accelerations come from differentiating position twice, which amplifies noise: injury criteria from video should be validated against an accelerometer before they are relied on. A grid of tracked points is not full-field DIC.
